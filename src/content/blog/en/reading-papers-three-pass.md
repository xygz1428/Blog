The most common illusion when reading a paper is "I understand it." When I closed the book, I realized that I couldn't tell the key points - the problem was not my memory, but the depth of my reading.

This method comes from the idea of ​​​​three readings. I made adjustments according to my own habits: **Every time there must be output**, and the time without output does not count.

## Pass 1: Draw structure only (10 minutes)

**Goal**: Know what problem this article is solving and what route it takes.

Without copying the content, just answer three questions:

1. What problem is it trying to solve?
2. What are others doing, and what are the shortcomings?
3. What method does it use and what is its summary in one sentence?

**Output**: A 3–5 line summary, written in your own words.

This time allows for a lot of ignorance. Just put a question mark when you encounter an unfamiliar term without digging deeper - digging deeper will make you stuck in the first section and unable to get out.

**Determine whether to read it a second time**: If this article has nothing to do with your topic, you can stop here. Most papers fall into this category.

## Second pass: only the derivation (1–2 hours)

**Goal**: Figure out how the conclusion came about.

Only focus on the areas marked with question marks on the first pass. The method is: **Close the original text and push it on the paper yourself**.

- Launched → Write down the key steps in one sentence
- Can’t push it out → Go back and look at it and use different colors to make it up
- Complete it and then close it and push it again.

The output this time is **derivation fragment**: how the formula came about, why the experiment was designed in this way, and why a certain parameter took this value.

**Note**: Don’t try to read every detail. Some implementation details are not useful for understanding the main line, so just mark them as "omitted".

## Third time: retell only (20 minutes)

**Goal**: Be able to explain things clearly to others.

Without reading your notes, try saying it orally:

- Can’t tell → Reply a second time
- You can tell it but it’s verbose → It means you haven’t grasped the core yet
- Can speak clearly in three sentences → Basically pass the test

A better approach is to write a 200-word introduction and pretend to explain it to others.

**Output**: A retellable summary and a judgment of "what can this article be used for?"

## The core of the three times method is not "three"

But **the output of each pass is different**:

| all over | output | time spent |
|---|---|---|
| one | 3–5 line summary | 10 minutes |
| two | Derivation fragment + Q&A | 1–2 hours |
| three | 200 words retelling | 20 minutes |

Many people only "read the paper" for the first time, but think they have completed it three times.

## Some practical suggestions

**1. Read the abstract, figures, and conclusion first**

These three parts have the highest information density. If you are not sure whether to go deeper after reading this, read the last paragraph of the introduction (which usually contains a list of contributions).

**2. Don’t read from the first section to the last section**

Look at the conclusion and diagram first, then go back and understand how you got it. Sequential reading can easily consume your patience in related work.

**3. Write down the "reasons for not reading"**

When abandoning a paper, you should also leave one sentence: why it is not relevant. There is no need to re-judge next time you encounter the same article.

**4. Use the same note template**

Fixed templates can reduce recording costs. What I use is: Question/Method/Key Conclusion/Relation to Me/To Be Verified.

## When not to use the three-pass method

- **Only one number**: Search directly, don’t read
- **Already Familiar Areas**: Skip to the second time
- **Purely want to get an overview**: The first pass is enough

The method serves the purpose, not the other way around.
