When faced with information overload, the first reaction is usually to "increase efficiency"—read faster, manage better, and filter smarter.

But the problem is not the processing speed, but the input volume itself.

## a simple arithmetic

Assume 3 hours of available attention per day.

- If input takes 5 hours to process → forever in debt, anxiety builds up
- If typing only takes 1 hour → 2 hours are left for practical work

**Increasing the processing speed can only reduce 5 hours to 4 hours, while reducing input can directly reduce it to 1 hour. **

## Subtraction 1: Remove the barriers instead of sorting them out

Favorites, read later, subscription lists – the thrill of organizing them is fake.

Because **organizing does not reduce the total amount**, it just turns 500 items into "500 classified items".

The really effective action is **delete**:

- Information sources that have not been opened for three months should be closed.
- If you have collected it but can’t remember why, delete it.
-Things that "may be useful in the future" are basically useless.

**Judgment Criteria**: If this message disappeared tomorrow, would I be affected? Most answers are no.

## Subtraction 2: Set a fixed window for the input

It's not "watch it whenever you have time", but "watch it for 30 minutes every day."

The reason is: **The flow of readily available information continues to interrupt deep work**. Every time it is interrupted, it takes more than ten minutes to re-enter the state.

Advantages of fixed windows:

- The number of interruptions has been reduced from dozens to one
- There is a boundary and it will not extend indefinitely
- Be more focused when watching (knowing that time is limited)

## Subtraction three: Distinguish between "consumption" and "production"

Reading information is consumption, writing and making things is production.

The marginal returns of consumption diminish very quickly - you will get about the same amount of new information if you browse for two hours as if you browse for 20 minutes.

The benefits of production are cumulative: after you finish writing an article or completing something, it will always exist.

**A simple test**: How many hours have I consumed and how many hours have I produced in the past week?

When the ratio is out of balance, it is more direct to reduce consumption than to improve efficiency.

## About "Fear of Missing Out"

The underlying fear of information overload is FOMO – the fear of missing out on something important.

But here’s the thing: **really important information is repeated**.

- Breaking news is discussed over and over again
- Key technological developments will appear in multiple channels
- Books worth reading will be recommended by different people

If something only appears once in one place and then disappears - it probably doesn't matter.

## a counterintuitive observation

After reducing the input, the output actually increased.

Not because there is more time (although there is a little more), but because of **attention continuum**. Deep work requires uninterrupted chunks of time, and information flow just chops it up.

## How to do it specifically

Start with these three items and you can complete them in a week:

1. **Uninstall 10** information sources that I haven’t read in three months
2. **Set a** fixed information window time
3. **Record the consumption/production time ratio for three days**

It doesn’t need to be done all at once, and it doesn’t need to be executed perfectly. **Reducing input by 30%, the effect is already obvious. **

## summary

| action | Common practice | More effective approach |
|---|---|---|
| Process information | Improve reading speed | Reduce input |
| Manage collection | Classification | Delete directly |
| Get information | Check anytime | fixed window |
| time allocation | squeeze time | Reduce consumption, increase production |

**The more information the better, but the more relevant the better. **
